# Whisper, Breath & Vocal Performance

Create intimate and believable vocal details that carry tension and emotion.

## Whisper types
Distinguish:
- **True whisper:** very low volume, little or no vocal fold vibration.
- **Breathy quiet speech:** softly voiced, intimate, still clearly articulated.
- **Hushed urgent speech:** quiet but tense and fast.
- **Fear whisper:** unstable breath, restrained volume, cautious phrasing.
- **Secretive whisper:** close, controlled, deliberate consonants.
- **Shocked whisper:** interrupted onset, breath catch, sparse words.

Do not write simply "[whisper]" everywhere. State the type, intention, intensity, and duration.

## Breath and vocalization vocabulary
Use only where motivated by the scene:
- inhale, shaky inhale, held breath, breath release
- gasp, sharp gasp, startled inhale
- exhale, sigh, relieved sigh
- breath tremor, trembling voice
- swallowed words, voice crack
- quiet sob, restrained sob, sobbing breath
- chuckle, nervous laugh, broken laugh
- cry, scream, shout, strained call
- throat catch, hesitant onset

## Timing and placement
- Mark whether a breath occurs before, between, or after words.
- Use silence before a reveal or after a frightening sound.
- Keep breaths short and natural; avoid placing a gasp after every sentence.
- For close-up scenes, use subtle mouth/breath detail; for distant scenes, prioritize clear readable vocal delivery.
- Do not confuse audible breathing with a constant breathy voice.

## Safety and performance quality
- Avoid directing actors to hyperventilate, scream at unsafe volume, or strain their voice.
- Suggest safe, controlled performance and sound design layers instead of physically forcing distress.
- Do not fabricate distress sounds that contradict the character's physical state.
